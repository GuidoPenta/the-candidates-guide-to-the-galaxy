## 0. Mindset before you start ([intro](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md))

- **Ignore other people's status.** If someone in a community flexes a huge salary, don't slip into
  anxiety or impostor syndrome: everyone has their own path, knowledge, network. Treat it as a hint
  for improvement, not proof you'll never make it.
- **Not everyone has to be a rockstar.** A "regular" dev with a normal 9-to-6 job at a "regular"
  company is fine. You don't need the hottest startup or the biggest paycheck.
- **Talking about money is normal.** Nobody works for free. It is not about being a mercenary: it's
  awareness, value, getting what's fair.

## 1. Principles — negotiation is a technical skill ([§1](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md#1-negotiation-is-a-technical-skill))

- **If you don't negotiate, you lose money. Always** — and usually without noticing.
- It is a **conversation, not a conflict**: two parties who want to collaborate but have
  (legitimately) different interests. **[IT]** "The company is not a non-profit": often its goal is
  to pay you the minimum needed to hire you; your job is to make your value perceived.
- It is not about tricking the other side: it's finding an agreement **sustainable for both**.
  **[IT]** It's communication but above all *analysis*: interests to align, constraints to
  understand, levers to use — problem solving. "If you optimize SQL queries, you can optimize
  your next RAL."
- **The meritocracy myth:** "if I do my job well someone will notice" is "COMPLETELY FALSE". Work is
  not a video-game quest where grinding eventually earns the legendary sword. Without awareness,
  visibility and negotiation your value stays invisible.
- **The cost of passivity:** every time you accept a salary "because it already seemed high", don't
  ask "so as not to seem greedy", or don't negotiate "for fear of losing the offer", you give away
  money, time and bargaining power. Over 5 years a dev who never negotiates can lose tens of
  thousands of euros. **[IT]** Handing over your payslip ("busta paga (!!!)") is listed among the
  ways you give value away.
- Negotiation is learnable: tools, strategies and mindset you can "learn, test, iterate" **[IT]**,
  like the rest of your work.

## 2. Preparation — know your market value ([§2](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md#2-before-the-interview-prepare-the-ground))

Do this **before** the interview — in fact before writing the CV or applying at all.

**Objective data (measurable, checkable online):**
- Glassdoor, Levels.fyi, salary surveys in tech communities.
- **[IT]** also techcompenso.com (Italian tech salaries), Payscale, LinkedIn Salary, asking in
  communities, reading online reviews, and **reading the company's financial statements**
  (*bilanci*) — "spoiler: they're often free and public. You don't want to get hired by a company
  that's going bankrupt, do you?"

**Subjective / personal factors:** how much do I need to live on? What RAL do I want to reach?
Remote, hybrid, on-site? Company car? Willing to travel? **[IT]** Mortgage? Kids? Dogs? Raccoons?
Combine objective and subjective into a **balanced numeric range** you want to be in.

**The company:** size, industry, growth stage and culture affect its ability and willingness to
offer certain packages.

**BATNA / walk-away point (skill-pack note):** the guide doesn't use the term, but repeatedly
implies it: avoid ultimatums "unless you're really ready to walk away", and declining a too-low
offer "is an act of lucidity, not arrogance". Define your minimum acceptable range and your
alternative (current job, other processes) before talking numbers.

## 3. "What's your current salary?" / "What are your expectations?" ([§2](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md#2-before-the-interview-prepare-the-ground), [§3](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md#3-play-your-cards-right))

**Current salary / payslip → don't disclose it.**
- Never give your current RAL, payslip or similar data before/at the interview. Reason: it lets the
  company anchor the offer to your past salary instead of your market value. **[IT]** "By giving
  these data you essentially lose 99% of your negotiating power."
- **Italy:** it is **legal** for a company to ask for your payslip ("many think it's illegal"), and
  it is **equally legitimate to refuse**.

**Expectations → state a range, and do it EARLY.**
- Talk money **from the first contact** (recruiter, HR, headhunter, whoever): "I'm looking for a
  range between x and y." This (1) shows you know what you want/are worth, (2) lets **you** set
  the numbers, (3) saves time by killing processes that would end in an RAL not in line with yours.
- One of the most common mistakes is avoiding pay until they bring it up: the later you discuss it,
  the higher the risk of misunderstandings and misaligned expectations.
- You don't have to give an exact number immediately, but at least clarify:
  - what range is being considered;
  - whether it matches your minimum expectations;
  - whether the company has room to move or is "budget-locked".
- Being clear about what you want doesn't make you arrogant — it makes you **"alignable"**, and
  helps them build a suitable offer. **[IT]** Presenting yourself as "fine for any role on any
  terms" conveys uncertainty; saying what you want and what motivates you is "real-time personal
  branding".

**Script (from the guide):**
> "Based on my current role and the benchmarks I've gathered, I'm looking for a range between 45K
> and 55K, depending on remote policy and benefits. What range do you usually offer for this type
> of position?"

Cited data: Payscale — not negotiating the starting salary costs about $1M over a career (US data,
but the compounding effect applies everywhere). Hired's State of Salaries — devs who state too-low
expectations get offers 10–15% below market average for the same role.

Also: **ask questions** in the interview — you're there to evaluate them too (see the
galactic-reverse-interview skill).

## 4. Evaluating the offer — total compensation ([§4](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md#4-serious-negotiation))

An offer is **the start** of the real negotiation, not the end. You don't have to say yes right
away. **[IT]** "Read it cold. Remember it's an offer, not a prize."

**What to look at before replying:**
- **RAL** (*Retribuzione Annua Lorda*, gross annual salary): watch out if it's unclear whether it
  includes variable pay or bonuses.
- **Bonus, stock, MBO:** guaranteed? variable? tied to objectives? discretionary?
- **Benefits:** meal vouchers (*buoni pasto*), health insurance, training budget, real or fake smart working.
- **Employment status (*inquadramento*):** are you hired as an employee (*dipendente*) or as a
  **fake freelancer with a VAT number (P.IVA)**?
- **Hours and flexibility:** full remote? real flexibility? **[IT]** or the classic "young and
  dynamic company" looking for people who work "without schedules" (aka: you stay until 8 pm every day)?

**What is negotiable? "ALMOST EVERYTHING":**
- RAL, obviously.
- Bonus/variable: ask for them to be revised, guaranteed, or converted into fixed pay.
- **Contract level (*livello contrattuale*, under the CCNL):** affects holidays, **TFR** (severance),
  maternity/paternity leave and future protections.
- Remote policy: full remote today? tomorrow? forever?
- Hours: flexibility, compressed hours, 4-day week — sometimes you can ask.
- Training: annual budget, dedicated time, conferences.
- Equipment: laptop, monitor, chair, setup budget.
- **Job title:** changes your future marketability.
- Onboarding and mentoring: is it planned? is it done well?

**Pro tip:** if they can't raise the RAL, ask for benefits with **real economic value**. A company
that says "we can't increase the salary but we really want you" should prove it with something concrete.

**Comparison example (from the guide):**
- Offer A: 40K RAL + full remote + €2,000 training budget + health insurance + top company laptop.
- Offer B: 40K RAL + 3 days on-site + no training + an old laptop ("*laptop de mi nonno*") + a pat on the back.
Same number on paper, not equivalent: A gives you more tools to grow, study and live better.

Cited data: Stack Overflow Developer Survey — 63% of devs would choose more flexibility/growth at
equal pay; 40% of those who negotiated the initial offer improved it. Levels.fyi — in US big tech,
good negotiation can bring +10% equity and +15–20% bonus. **[IT] Italy:** "a reasoned counter-offer
can often unlock 5–10K more (if well argued)."

**Offer comparison checklist (skill-pack note, built from the list above):** for each offer record
RAL, fixed vs variable split and how variable is decided, CCNL and level, employee vs P.IVA,
benefits with a € value, remote days, hours, training budget, equipment, title, onboarding.

## 5. The counter-offer ([§5](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md#5-the-counteroffer))

A counter-offer is not ingratitude, rudeness or arrogance — it's a normal, healthy part of any
negotiation. Whoever makes an offer expects you may negotiate; doing it shows you know the market
and aren't desperate for "anything, as long as you hire me".

Rules:
- **Be specific.** Not "I'd like a bit more" but: "I expected an RAL closer to 55K, based on the
  role, my background and the benchmarks I collected."
- **Argue, don't beg.** You're bringing data and a proposal. Highlight experience, impact,
  autonomy, **[IT]** rare know-how.
- **Be open / assertive, not a wall.** "I'd like to find a middle ground that makes the most of
  this opportunity for both of us."
- **No ultimatums** unless you are truly ready to walk away. Avoid "10K more or nothing". Better:
  > "The only thing stopping me from saying yes today is the economic side: if we could get closer
  > to X, I'd gladly accept."
- **Put it in writing.** If the counter-offer happens by phone or video call, send a short written recap.

**Email template (adapted from the guide):**
> Hi [Name],
> thank you for the offer and for your openness throughout the process.
> I'm very interested in the project and would be happy to join [company].
> I've carefully reviewed the terms and, compared to my expectations (based on role,
> responsibilities and market benchmarks), I was hoping for a slightly higher offer: ideally
> around [x–y]K.
> If we could get closer to that range — also considering benefits or alternatives to pure
> salary — I'd be happy to accept and start working together.
> Thanks again, [Your name]

Why it works: empathetic, leaves room, shows the reasoning, keeps it a conversation between adults
who both want a good compromise.

**If they say no — two paths:**
- The project excites you and the offer is still decent → accept, and **set a 6- or 12-month RAL
  review** from the start.
- What they recognize is too far from what you deserve → declining "is an act of lucidity, not arrogance".

## 6. Advanced technique — for anxious negotiators ([§7](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md#7-bonus-advanced-negotiation))

The fear of seeming "too much", "ungrateful", "difficult" is real, and nobody teaches negotiation
(Guido: it took him years of trial and error). Talking about money is NOT vulgar.

1. **Mindset:** you're not asking a favor, you're exploring an agreement. Think "we're figuring out
   how to make this collaboration work", not "please take me and pay me".
2. **"Gentle disarmament":** soften the tone, not the message. **[IT]** Examples: "I know it may
   sound like a significant figure, but I think it reflects the value and the current market well"
   / "I realize it's a demanding request, which is exactly why I wanted to talk about it openly."
3. **Practice out loud** — in front of a mirror or a trusted person: "I'd like to ask for a raise",
   "I expected an RAL closer to 50K", "According to my research, for a role with these
   responsibilities the average is…". **[IT]** Guido has interviewed many people who, when money
   came up, went "uhm… I don't know… you decide".
4. **Leave room for silence:** state your request, then stop talking. Listen to the room/call, the
   non-verbal cues. **[IT]** Never add "…I mean, obviously if it's not possible that's fine too" —
   that is "the TOTAL death of the negotiation".
5. **Aim for balance, not victory:** a sustainable agreement where you feel valued and they feel
   you're giving the right amount.

## 7. Junior vs mid vs senior ([§8](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md#8-bonus-2-junior-vs-senior))

Everyone can and should negotiate; adapt *how* and *what* you bring. **[IT]** Expectations must be
realistic, "but never lowballed".

- **Junior (0–2 years):** feeling weaker is normal, but don't accept any offer as if it were a
  favor — start building a healthy relationship with negotiation now. Leverage: education (serious
  bootcamp, good university, technical master, intensive online courses); **[IT]** personal
  projects (an active GitHub with proper projects — Guido begs juniors to build one), freelance
  work, open-source contributions; soft skills (eagerness to learn, consistency, communication,
  punctuality, clarity — "not bonuses: fundamentals"). Goal: balance between learning and a
  dignified minimum salary, not squeezing the highest RAL. **[IT]** Don't fixate on full remote
  either (Guido is an "EXTREME supporter" of full remote, but asks: are you sure you can work
  remotely yet?). Legitimate example: "I expected an RAL around 26–28K, in line with what I've
  seen for this type of role."
- **Senior:** you sell results, not potential: delivery on critical projects, mentoring, architectural
  decisions with business impact, metrics (uptime, automation, performance, cost optimization).
  More leverage to compare offers; rare skills (leadership, vertical stacks, complex domains)
  justify a proposal touching **RAL, bonus, equity, flexibility, role prospects, influence on
  product/strategy**.
- **Mid:** the ambiguous middle — more experience than a junior, not all a senior's levers. Work on
  two fronts: collect evidence (projects, feedback, results) and prepare the jump, possibly waiting
  for the right moment (new project, internal promotion, an external offer that unblocks things).

## 8. Freelance / P.IVA ([§9](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/README.md#9-bonus-track-3-freelance))

- Negotiation is part of professional survival: your hourly/daily/project rate must reflect the
  value you bring, **the costs you bear and the risk you take**.
- **Most common mistake: thinking like an employee with a VAT number.** "We pay you €2,000/month,
  you're freelance but work only for us, fixed hours, agreed holidays, no external clients" is not
  freelancing: **[IT]** it's disguised subordinate employment, and often illegal. Real freelancing
  means contractual freedom, autonomous management, choosing clients, pace and methods — and that
  has a price.
- **Rate calculation [IT]:** don't copy what others ask on LinkedIn (comparing in communities is
  healthy, but only a starting point). Start from real costs: taxes, social contributions, holidays,
  sick days, training, tools, client management. Add the value of your time and expertise, plus the
  fact that nobody pays your downtime. The result will often be higher than you expect — "and it must be".
- Don't reason in "RAL converted to P.IVA". The right question: "how much do I need to earn to live
  well, work sustainably and grow?" — and, if necessary, refuse clients who don't respect your role.

## 9. Asking for a raise (after hiring) ([§6](https://github.com/GuidoPenta/galactic-negotiation-guide-for-developers/blob/main/docs/english.md#6-how-to-ask-for-a-raise))

**When it makes sense:** after 12 months without automatic raises; after a role/responsibility
change (e.g. dev → tech lead, even if the contract didn't change); after measurable impact; after a
positive annual review not followed by a pay adjustment; when you have **real** competitive external
offers and want to stay (not blackmail — handle with tact).

**When not:** 3 months in, onboarding unfinished; only because you found out a colleague earns more
("fair to be angry, but you need a strategy"); "more money or I leave" without a real plan B
("leave rage-quitting to video games"); during a deep company crisis.

**How:** prepare a mini dossier (slides/Excel/Canva/whatever) for the right moment and people:
(1) what you did when you joined vs today; (2) projects, results, metrics; (3) positive feedback
(clients, managers, team); (4) current salary benchmarks for your role. Then a clear ask **[IT]**:
> "I'd like to discuss a possible salary adjustment. Over the last 12 months my role has grown a
> lot: I'm managing X, I delivered Y, and based on updated benchmarks a more aligned RAL would be
> between Z and Z+5K. Can we discuss it?"

If they say "in three months" → get a written follow-up in the calendar. If "we'll talk later" →
ask *when exactly*. **[IT]** "'Later' doesn't pay the rent."

**If the answer is no:** (1) ask why, constructively: "can you help me understand what's needed to
get there in the coming months?"; (2) "no budget now" (a classic) → is it temporary, when is the next
decision window?; (3) "you're still missing X" → have X spelled out, ideally in writing —
**"scripta manent, ALWAYS"**; (4) ask for a written follow-up / 3-month check-in; (5) put the date in
the calendar yourself if needed.

**If it will never happen:** stay for other reasons (learning, stability, project) knowing you won't
grow financially there, or look around — calmly, with the clarity of someone who tried everything
in a structured way.

Cited data: Glassdoor — 70% of people who ask for a raise get something; Hays Italia — 2024
retention raises averaged 5–12%, and those who ask get more than those who wait; Payscale —
changing jobs for money yields ~15% on average, while staying 2+ years without renegotiating often
means losing purchasing power.

## 10. Written confirmation — "scripta manent"

A recurring rule across the guide:
- recap every phone/video negotiation in a short written message;
- get promises (review dates, "missing X", future raises) in writing;
- **(skill-pack note)** before resigning from a current job, get the final offer in writing
  (RAL, level/CCNL, variable, benefits, remote policy, start date). The guide doesn't spell this
  step out, but it follows directly from its "always get it in writing" principle.

## 11. Multiple offers

The guide covers this only indirectly:
- seniors "have more leverage to compare offers";
- real competitive external offers are a legitimate trigger for a raise conversation, but "not
  blackmail" and only if you actually want to stay;
- compare offers on total package, not RAL alone (Offer A vs B example).
**(skill-pack note)** No specific script for playing offers against each other exists in the
guide; the skill should not invent one in Guido's name. Apply the same rules: be honest and
specific, no bluffing (offers must be real), no ultimatums you won't honor.

## 12. Mistakes to avoid (collected from the guide)

1. Not negotiating at all / accepting on the spot.
2. Disclosing current RAL or payslip.
3. Waiting for them to bring up money; discussing it late.
4. Stating too-low expectations (anchors you 10–15% below market).
5. Judging an offer by RAL alone; ignoring variable conditions, level, contract type, remote reality.
6. Accepting a fake-freelance P.IVA arrangement as if it were a job.
7. Vague asks ("a bit more"), begging instead of arguing with data.
8. Ultimatums you're not ready to honor.
9. Filling the silence after your ask or backpedaling ("if not, that's fine too").
10. Verbal-only agreements; "we'll talk later" with no date.
11. Asking for a raise at the wrong time (3 months in, company in crisis, only because of a colleague's salary).
12. Freelancers: pricing by copying LinkedIn rates or converting an RAL.
13. Comparing yourself with salary-flexers in communities and feeling like an impostor.
